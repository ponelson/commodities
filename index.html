A commodity price is a map of physical constraints

Commodity markets look like screens full of prices, but the screen is the final layer. Underneath sit mines, wells, farms, processors, warehouses, ships, pipelines, power grids, financing, and contracts. This guide starts at zero and builds the system upward—from physical balance and inventory to basis, curves, hedging, investable instruments, and the claims most likely to mislead.

01 / Orientation

A commodity is a specification, not a story

The market does not buy “wheat,” “copper,” or “oil” in the abstract. It buys a defined grade, at a defined place, in a defined month, with rules for delivery and settlement.

Thing

Physical good

A fungible input: barrels, tonnes, bushels, pounds, megawatt-hours. Chemistry and quality still matter.

Place

Delivery point

A price belongs somewhere. Moving material between origins and consumers creates basis, freight, and congestion.

Time

Delivery month

Storage links today to tomorrow. The curve records the price of time, scarcity, financing, and inventory.

Rules

Contract

Lot size, grade, location, expiry, settlement, and force-majeure terms turn a product into a tradable instrument.

The sentence to keep

A commodity price is the clearing price for one specification, at one location, for one moment in time. Change any of those nouns and you may be looking at a different market.

02 / The map

Four families, four physical logics

The common language is price, but the underlying constraints are different enough that each family deserves its own mental model.

Energy

Flows through networks

Oil can be stored and shipped; gas depends more heavily on pipelines and liquefaction; electricity must be balanced almost instantly. Transport capacity often defines the market.

Metals

Stocks above ground matter

Base metals are industrial inputs, precious metals also behave as monetary assets, and bulk minerals are dominated by logistics. Grade, recovery, and treatment charges connect mine economics to refined price.

Agriculture

Biology meets weather

Planting cycles, yields, harvest windows, spoilage, and substitution create sharp seasonal balances. A forecast error cannot be fixed until the next crop.

Livestock & softs

Inventories can grow—or rot

Cattle and hogs have biological lags. Coffee, cocoa, sugar, and cotton combine concentrated geography with weather, labor, and disease risk.

03 / Physical balance

Price begins with a mass balance

Start with what is available, add what arrives, subtract what is consumed, and reconcile the difference in inventory. Almost every commodity argument is a disagreement about one of those terms.

Ending stocks = Beginning stocks + Production + Imports − Consumption − Exports − Losses

Supply responds slowly

Mines take years, wells decline, crops wait for seasons, and processing capacity is specialized. High prices do not summon immediate supply.

Demand can be stubborn

Factories cannot redesign products overnight, utilities must keep systems running, and food demand is essential. Short-run elasticity is often low.

Inventory is the shock absorber

Stocks bridge timing gaps. When usable inventory is thin, a small disruption can require a large price move to destroy demand or attract supply.

Read balances skeptically. “Inventory” may exclude material in transit, strategic reserves, unreported private stocks, or metal unavailable at today’s price. The headline number is not always the accessible number.
04 / Time

The futures curve is a market for storage

A strip of monthly prices shows how the market values delivery through time. It is not a simple forecast.

Contango

Later delivery trades above prompt delivery. Carry may reflect financing, storage, insurance, and ample current supply. If the spread covers all costs, inventory can be bought, stored, and hedged.

Backwardation

Prompt delivery trades above later delivery. Immediate possession has value: inventories may be scarce, buyers urgent, or constraints binding. The implied convenience yield is high.

05 / Space & quality

Basis is where the physical market speaks

Basis is the difference between a local cash price and a chosen benchmark. It absorbs freight, quality, timing, taxes, congestion, and local bargaining power.

06 / Participants

Commercial users transfer risk; investors warehouse it

The same futures contract can be prudent insurance for one participant and a directional bet for another.

Hedger

Producer

Sells futures or swaps to lock a selling price. The hedge reduces price risk but creates basis risk and can require cash collateral.

Hedger

Consumer

Buys futures or swaps to lock an input cost. Airlines, food manufacturers, utilities, and fabricators care about margin, not just price direction.

Risk capital

Trader or investor

Takes price, curve, volatility, or relative-value exposure. Their liquidity helps hedgers transact, but leverage can amplify forced flows.

Hedge effectiveness. A perfect hedge matches commodity, grade, location, volume, and timing. Real hedges rarely match all five, so the residual is basis risk.
07 / Market instruments

Choose the exposure before choosing the ticker

Spot cargoes, futures, options, swaps, producer equities, royalties, and funds can all be called “commodity exposure.” Their payoff mechanics are not interchangeable.

08 / Return anatomy

A futures return has more than one engine

For a collateralized futures position, total return is approximately the move in the contract, plus the gain or loss from replacing it as expiry approaches, plus interest earned on collateral.

Total return ≈ Spot/contract return + Roll yield + Collateral yield − fees

Price return

The change in the held futures contract. It can resemble spot over short horizons but is not identical.

Roll yield

The effect of selling the expiring contract and buying a later one. Persistent contango is a headwind; backwardation can be a tailwind.

Collateral yield

Futures require margin rather than full notional funding, so uncommitted cash can earn a short-term rate.

09 / Research workflow

Build the view from the physical system outward

A durable thesis connects a balance, a bottleneck, a price mechanism, and a falsifiable timeline.

Write down exactly what the quoted price represents. Identify the deliverable grades, location, lot size, last trading day, and whether settlement is physical or financial.
Separate nameplate capacity from effective supply. Track maintenance, outages, yields, imports, exports, losses, and inventory by location and usability.
Ask which producer must enter, which consumer must exit, or which trade route must open to clear the market. That unit anchors the price mechanism.
Specify what should happen to inventories, basis, spreads, utilization, and price—and by when. A thesis that cannot fail is a narrative, not analysis.
10 / Failure modes

The promotional playbook

Commodity stories become dangerous when a true macro observation is used to skip the hard questions of timing, substitution, incentive, and investable exposure.

“Structural shortage” with no price response

High prices ration demand, invite substitution, release scrap, improve recovery, and finance supply. Ask what prevents each response.

Demand in percentages, supply in adjectives

Convert both sides into the same physical unit and year. Small bases make dramatic growth rates easy to manufacture.

Benchmark price equals realized price

Adjust for grade, location, payability, freight, treatment, timing, and hedges. Revenue is earned at the realized net price.

Spot thesis, equity instrument

A producer adds operating cost, capex, tax, geology, jurisdiction, leverage, dilution, and management. Those may dominate the commodity view.

11 / Vocabulary

The words, defined

Filter the core language of physical and derivatives markets.

Basis

Local cash price minus the chosen futures benchmark, with convention checked carefully.

Backwardation

A curve in which nearby delivery is priced above later delivery.

Contango

A curve in which later delivery is priced above nearby delivery.

Convenience yield

The non-cash benefit of holding usable physical inventory.

Crack / crush spread

A modeled processing margin between an input commodity and its outputs.

Hedge

A position intended to offset an existing commercial or financial exposure.

Initial margin

Collateral posted to open a derivatives position; it is not the position’s purchase price.

Open interest

The number of outstanding contracts, distinct from daily trading volume.

Roll yield

The return effect of replacing an expiring futures contract with a later one.

Spot

The market for immediate or near-immediate delivery under local convention.

Variation margin

Cash transferred as a futures position is marked to market.

Warehouse receipt

A document or electronic record representing deliverable material in approved storage.

12 / Retain

Twelve things worth keeping

  1. Define the commodity by grade, place, month, and contract.
  2. Start every view with a physical balance.
  3. Accessible inventory matters more than headline inventory.
  4. Low short-run elasticity makes small imbalances move price sharply.
  5. The curve prices time and scarcity; it is not a consensus forecast.
  6. Basis carries local information that the benchmark cannot.
  7. The marginal unit—not the average unit—usually anchors price.
  8. A hedge replaces price risk with basis, liquidity, and counterparty risks.
  9. Futures returns include roll and collateral effects.
  10. Producer equities are businesses, not barrels or tonnes.
  11. Every shortage thesis needs a reason supply, substitution, or demand response fails.
  12. A good thesis states what would prove it wrong.
13 / Further study

Where to go next

Contract rules and official statistical systems are the primary sources. Use commentary to generate questions, then verify them against the underlying market.

Exchange rulebooks

Contract specifications, delivery procedures, position limits, calendars, and settlement methodology.

Government statistics

Energy balances, crop reports, customs data, mine production, trade, stocks, and industrial consumption.

Industry institutions

Technical definitions, benchmark methodologies, warehouse data, shipping information, and processing economics.

Standing caveat. This is an educational framework, not investment, trading, legal, or tax advice. Contract specifications and market conventions change; verify the current rulebook before acting.
